# What makes a speaker captivating

This is the reference the analyzer encodes. It exists because thresholds like "130–165 words per
minute" are worthless without a theory of *why* anything matters, and because almost everything
worth knowing about speaking was worked out between 350 BCE and 95 CE and has been rediscovered,
under new names, roughly once a generation since.

## 1. The premise: it is a craft, not a gift

Aristotle opens the *Rhetoric* (c. 350 BCE) by defining rhetoric as "the faculty of observing in any
given case the available means of persuasion." A *faculty* — a trainable capacity with a method.
This is the founding move of the whole tradition, and it is still the most important thing to tell a
nervous client: captivating speakers are manufactured, not discovered.

The transmitted story about Demosthenes makes the point at the level of legend. Born with a speech
impediment and a weak voice, he supposedly declaimed with pebbles in his mouth, practiced against
the noise of the surf to build volume, built an underground study, and shaved half his head so that
shame would keep him indoors practicing. Asked what came first in oratory, he answered "Delivery."
Second? "Delivery." Third? "Delivery." Whether or not the details are true, what got transmitted for
2,400 years is the claim that elite delivery is built through deliberate, physically specific
practice — and that delivery outranks content in its effect on an audience.

## 2. Aristotle: three proofs, and why the audience must do the work

Aristotle divides persuasion into three *artistic* proofs — artistic because the speaker creates
them, as opposed to evidence that merely exists.

**Ethos** — the character the speaker projects. Aristotle's striking claim is that ethos must be
produced *by the speech itself*, not carried in from prior reputation. Credibility is something you
build in the first two minutes, in front of the audience, or not at all.

**Pathos** — putting the audience into a particular state of feeling. Book II of the *Rhetoric* is
mostly a catalogue of emotions (anger, calm, fear, shame, pity, indignation, envy) with the precise
conditions that produce each. Aristotle spends more space on the audience's emotional machinery than
on logic, which tells you where he thought the leverage was.

**Logos** — reasoning, of which the characteristic rhetorical form is the **enthymeme**: a syllogism
with a premise deliberately left out for the audience to supply. This is the single most
underrated idea in the tradition. When the listener completes the argument, they are persuaded by
their own reasoning rather than yours, and people do not argue with conclusions they reached
themselves. Every "show, don't tell" instruction ever given is a restatement of the enthymeme.

Aristotle on style: clarity first, then metaphor. "The greatest thing by far is to be a master of
metaphor... it is a sign of genius, since a good metaphor implies an intuitive perception of the
similarity in dissimilars." He also warns that prose should have *rhythm but not meter* — regular
enough to feel shaped, irregular enough not to sound like verse. That is a variance instruction, and
the analyzer measures it as sentence-length contrast.

## 3. Cicero: the five canons, and modulation as the mark of mastery

Cicero's five canons (*De Oratore*, 55 BCE) still organize the field:

| Canon | What it covers | Where it lives in this tool |
|---|---|---|
| **Inventio** | finding the arguments | not measurable; the coach's job |
| **Dispositio** | arrangement | opening/closing shape, signposting, throughline |
| **Elocutio** | style and figures | the rhetoric module: anaphora, tricolon, antithesis, concreteness |
| **Memoria** | memorization | visible indirectly: hesitation pauses and restarts betray an unrehearsed passage |
| **Pronuntiatio** | delivery — voice and gesture | the acoustic modules: volume, pitch, pace, pause, tone |

Cicero also names three duties of the orator — *docere* (to teach), *delectare* (to delight),
*movere* (to move) — matched to three styles: plain, middle, grand. His crucial point is that a
great speech is not written in one of these styles; it **modulates between them**. The plain style
carries the explanation, the grand style carries the peroration, and the movement between them is
what keeps an audience awake.

This is, in modern terms, an instruction to maximize variance. It is the same advice as "vary your
pitch," "vary your pace," "vary your sentence length" — and it is why the single most reliable
finding in this whole analyzer is that **flatness in any dimension is the universal failure mode**.

Quintilian (*Institutio Oratoria*, c. 95 CE) systematized all of it into twelve books and defined
the orator as *vir bonus dicendi peritus* — "a good man skilled in speaking," insisting that
character is part of the craft rather than a separate question. His Book XI treats voice and gesture
in terms a modern coach would recognize: volume, pitch, pace, and pause, with the repeated
observation that "the ear demands change."

## 4. The figures that survived, and why they work

Ancient handbooks catalogued hundreds of figures. A handful do nearly all the work, and each has a
plausible cognitive reason for surviving.

**Anaphora** — repetition at the start of successive clauses. "I have a dream." "We shall fight on
the beaches." "Let both sides..." Repetition sets up a prosodic expectation; by the third instance
the audience is anticipating the shape, and anticipation that gets satisfied is pleasurable.
It also makes the content dramatically easier to remember. Notably, the "I have a dream" passage was
not in King's prepared text — he improvised it over an anaphoric frame he had used before, after
Mahalia Jackson called out "Tell them about the dream, Martin!" The frame is what made improvisation
possible.

**Tricolon** — three parallel elements. "Veni, vidi, vici." "Life, liberty, and the pursuit of
happiness." "Government of the people, by the people, for the people" (tricolon *and* epistrophe).
Three is the smallest number that establishes a pattern: two is a coincidence, four is a list.

**Antithesis and chiasmus** — balanced opposition, and the ABBA reversal. "Ask not what your country
can do for you — ask what you can do for your country." The mind holds two structures in parallel
and the reversal produces a small click of resolution that *feels like insight*. This is worth
saying plainly to a client: the feeling is produced by the syntax, not necessarily by the content.
It is the most powerful and the most easily abused figure in the set.

**Erotema** — the rhetorical question. Cicero opens the First Catilinarian with a barrage of them
("How long, Catiline, will you go on abusing our patience?"). A question converts a listener from
receiving to answering. It is the enthymeme applied to attention.

## 5. What modern practice added

The moderns added very little theory and a great deal of useful empirical constraint.

**The throughline.** Chris Anderson's editorial rule at TED is that a talk must have one idea,
statable in about fifteen words, that every element connects to. Talks fail because they contain
four ideas, not because they contain a weak one. This is Cicero's *dispositio* with a hard budget.

**Oscillation.** Nancy Duarte's analysis of great speeches found that they alternate between "what
is" and "what could be" — building tension and releasing it, repeatedly, ending on what she calls
the new bliss. She found the same shape in King's Dream speech and in Steve Jobs' iPhone launch.
The oscillation is not decoration; it *is* the engagement mechanism, and it is Cicero's modulation
of styles at the level of structure rather than sentence.

**The curiosity gap.** George Loewenstein's account of curiosity as a felt gap between what I know
and what I want to know explains why great openings *open a loop* rather than announcing a topic.
"Today I'm going to talk about vulnerability" closes the loop. "I had a breakdown, and my therapist
called it a spiritual awakening" opens it.

**Concreteness.** The Heath brothers' central obstacle is the Curse of Knowledge: once you know
something you cannot remember not knowing it, so you speak in the compressed abstractions that only
make sense to people who already agree. The antidote is a specific person, in a specific place,
doing a specific thing. Nobody can picture "organizational complacency." Everyone can picture a
runner who trained the same as always and came in last.

**Steve Jobs** is the most-studied modern case and is almost entirely classical in technique:
- Tricolon constantly, often nested: "an iPod, a phone, and an internet communicator" — repeated
  three times, accelerating, before the reveal that they are one device.
- Analogy instead of specification: "1,000 songs in your pocket," never "5GB."
- A villain — complexity, the beige box, IBM. Conflict is what makes exposition into narrative.
- Long, unhurried pauses, frequently over two seconds, always *after* a line rather than inside one.
- The 2005 Stanford commencement address is a textbook *dispositio*: explicit signposting ("Today I
  want to tell you three stories from my life. That's it. No big deal. Just three stories."),
  a tricolon of narratives, each closing on one short line, and a peroration built from four words
  designed to be repeated: "Stay hungry. Stay foolish."

## 6. The synthesis: six mechanisms

Everything above collapses into six things a captivating speaker does. Each one is either measured
by this tool or coachable directly from what it measures.

1. **Open a gap.** The first thirty seconds pose something unresolved — a question, a scene, a
   number that should not be true. Announcing a topic closes the loop before it opens.
2. **Trade abstraction for image.** The audience can only feel what it can picture. One named
   person doing one specific thing outperforms any amount of correct generalization.
3. **Build a pattern, then break it.** Anaphora and tricolon create rhythm; the break in the pattern
   is where the point lands. This works at every scale, from the clause to the whole talk.
4. **Oscillate.** Loud and soft, fast and slow, long sentence and four-word sentence, tension and
   release, what-is and what-could-be. Cicero's modulation. Flatness is the universal failure.
5. **Make them do the work.** Enthymeme, rhetorical question, the unfinished implication. Persuasion
   the listener performs themselves is the only kind that survives contact with disagreement.
6. **Land it, then stop.** Silence after the point, not before the next word. Decide the one
   sentence you want repeated in the parking lot, and end on it.

The negative version is more useful for coaching, because it is what the tool actually detects:
a speaker fails by being **flat** (pitch, volume, pace, sentence length, structure), **abstract**
(no image, no story, no name), and **unlanded** (the point arrives and immediately gets covered by
the next sentence, with the pauses falling mid-clause instead of after the full stop).
